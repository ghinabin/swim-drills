## Evidence and its limits

1. **Warm up, but individualise it.** A randomized study of 20 competitive swimmers found faster average 100 m performance after their usual warm-up, although three swimmers were faster without it. It compared warm-up versus none, not buoy-first versus fist-first. Its 1,000 m warm-up is not a prescription for your much shorter sessions. [Neiva et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23579194/).
2. **More preparation is not automatically better.** In a randomized crossover study of 11 national-level male swimmers, 600 m and 1,200 m warm-ups preceded faster 100 m swims than 1,800 m. This supports controlling preparation load; it does not establish an ideal distance for your 50 m. [Neiva et al., 2015](https://ubibliorum.ubi.pt/entities/publication/f518fa47-be21-49de-98b2-a072c5fdf9ed).
3. **Fatigue can change technique.** An exhaustive front-crawl study in 15 male swimmers found reduced stroke length and force measures. This was exhaustive swimming at a speed based on a 400 m test, not a direct experiment on your drill order. Prioritising fresh starts and speed is a coaching inference from this evidence, not a proven universal rule. [Bassan et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25848804/).
4. **Match preparation to the main set.** USMS coaching guidance recommends relevant preparation before the main work and permits moving drills within a session according to its purpose. That supports a normal-freestyle transition before race-specific swimming; it does not prove the exact buoy/fist combination below. [Matt Donovan, USMS](https://www.usms.org/fitness-and-training/articles-and-videos/articles/how-to-write-a-practice-when-training-alone).
5. **Preserve recovery for speed.** USMS distinguishes long-rest maximum-speed work from shorter-rest endurance work. Reordering cannot compensate for insufficient recovery. [Terry Heggy, USMS](https://www.usms.org/fitness-and-training/articles-and-videos/articles/how-to-write-a-swimming-workout).

The exact daily sequences below are my recommendations based on those principles and the app's session goals. No retrieved study directly compares your specific buoy → fist → starts → 50 m sequence against an alternative. Research on trained swimmers over 100 m does not establish a guaranteed improvement for your 50 m.

## Your example: buoy → fist → starts → main set

This is acceptable when the buoy/fist work is easy and you arrive at the starts warm and fresh. Buoy before fist is not inherently wrong. The useful adjustment is **easy warm-up → brief catch work → normal freestyle/build → controlled starts → recovery → timed 50s → easy finish**.

For September 18 and 25, replace the existing eight 25 m drill lengths with **2 × 25 smooth buoy + 2 × (25 fist + 25 normal freestyle) + 2 × 25 progressive normal freestyle**. This remains 200 m. The last two lengths build smoothly, not all-out. October 2's four lengths can become **25 buoy + 25 fist + 25 normal freestyle + 25 progressive freestyle**, still 100 m. These are practical options, not scientifically validated optimal ratios.

Keep the existing controlled start counts only while they feel preparatory. Recover before the first timed 50 and maintain at least five minutes between the two Friday tests. The app's “Start block” means a block of practice; weekday starts use a permitted deck dive or push, not Saturday's starting platform.

On September 18 retain the fatigue check after the September 17 workout: if not recovered, substitute easy swimming for both tests. The push-first, deck-second order serves baseline tracking and rehearsal; it is not a controlled experiment comparing start types, because order and fatigue can influence the second swim.
